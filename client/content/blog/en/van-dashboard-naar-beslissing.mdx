## A familiar picture

Forty-two dashboards. Eleven of them opened at least once a month. Two of them opened by more than three people. And when you ask what decision changed because of any of them, the room goes quiet.

This is not a tooling failure. The tooling is fine.

## The missing question

Most dashboards start with "what data do we have?" The useful ones start with **"what decision does someone make on a recurring basis, and what would change their mind?"**

Those produce very different artefacts. The first gives you a wall of charts. The second gives you three numbers and a threshold.

## Three tests before you build

1. **Who is the decision-maker?** A name, not a department. If you cannot name them, you are building a report for an audience that does not exist.
2. **What is the cadence?** Daily operational calls need different data than a quarterly investment decision. Cadence sets the granularity, the freshness requirement, and the cost.
3. **What action follows?** If every possible value of the metric leads to the same action, the metric is decoration.

## Design for the decision, not the data

A dashboard supporting a weekly stock decision does not need twelve months of history on screen. It needs: current cover in days, the trend against last week, and a clear flag for the SKUs that fall below threshold. Everything else is context that can live one click away.

Compression is the work. Anyone can put every column on a page.

## The part nobody wants to hear

Some decisions do not need a dashboard at all. A weekly alert in the channel where the team already works — "these six SKUs dropped below two weeks of cover" — beats a dashboard nobody opens. The output of a data project does not have to be a dashboard. It has to be a decision made better.

## What to do with the other forty

Archive them. Track what people actually open for a month, keep what survives, and rebuild the rest around a named decision. A small set of trusted views beats a large set that people quietly stopped believing.
